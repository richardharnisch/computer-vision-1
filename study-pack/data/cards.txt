# Camera and image formation
camera | core | Where is the origin of the pinhole camera coordinate frame? | At the optical centre, also called the pinhole or centre of projection. | HC1b p.24; S1
camera | core | What is focal length in the ideal pinhole projection model? | The distance from the optical centre to the model's image plane, expressed in physical units or converted to pixels in K. | HC1b pp.21–26
camera | core | What is the virtual-image-plane pinhole projection formula? | For camera point [X,Y,Z] with Z>0: x=f*X/Z and y=f*Y/Z. The virtual image plane is in front of the pinhole at Z=f. | HC1b pp.24–26; S1
camera | core | How do projection signs differ for the physical image plane behind the pinhole? | With the same axes and a plane at Z=-f, x=-f*X/Z and y=-f*Y/Z. The physical image is inverted. Always check the convention. | HC1b p.24; S1
camera | core | What determines apparent size in perspective projection? | Projected size is proportional to focal length and physical size, and inversely proportional to distance/depth for a fronto-parallel object. | HC1b pp.25–27
camera | core | What happens to apparent object size if depth doubles and focal length stays fixed? | It halves for the ideal fronto-parallel pinhole setting. The image alone does not distinguish size from distance. | HC1b pp.25–27
camera | breadth | What happens to field of view when focal length increases for a fixed sensor? | Field of view decreases; projected object size increases. | HC1b pp.21–22
camera | breadth | Give the field-of-view formula for a centred pinhole sensor of width W. | Horizontal FOV=2*atan(W/(2*f)), with W and f in matching units. A corresponding vertical formula uses sensor height. | S1; Forsyth–Ponce camera chapter
camera | breadth | What is the aperture-size trade-off in a simple pinhole camera? | A larger hole admits more light but increases geometric blur. A very small hole admits little light and eventually suffers diffraction. | HC1b pp.7–20
camera | breadth | How does lens aperture size affect depth of field? | A smaller aperture generally increases depth of field; a larger aperture decreases it, with other camera settings fixed. | HC1b pp.16–20
camera | breadth | What is depth of field? | The range of scene depths that appear acceptably sharp for a given focus and camera setting. | HC1b pp.16–18
camera | core | How is a 2D Cartesian point converted to homogeneous coordinates? | [x,y] -> [x,y,1]. Any nonzero common scale represents the same finite point. | HC1b pp.32–34; S1
camera | core | How is a finite homogeneous point [a,b,w] converted to Cartesian coordinates? | [a/w,b/w], provided w is nonzero. Do not discard the third coordinate without dividing. | HC1b pp.33–34
camera | core | What does homogeneous-coordinate scale invariance mean? | x and alpha*x represent the same projective point for any nonzero alpha. This removes one effective degree of freedom. | HC1b p.34; HC4a p.22
camera | breadth | What does a homogeneous point with last coordinate zero represent? | A point at infinity, encoding a direction rather than a finite Cartesian location. It cannot be dehomogenized by ordinary division. | HC1b pp.28–34; Szeliski §2.1
camera | core | Why use homogeneous coordinates for geometric transformations? | They express translation and projective mappings by matrix multiplication and allow transformations to be composed consistently. | HC1b pp.32–45; HC4a
camera | core | Write the general 3 by 3 intrinsic matrix K. | K=[[fx,s,cx],[0,fy,cy],[0,0,1]]. fx,fy are focal scales in pixels; s is skew; cx,cy are principal-point coordinates. For orthogonal sensor axes with physical pixel pitches px,py, fx=f/px and fy=f/py in consistent units. | HC1b pp.35–45; Szeliski §2.1.4
camera | core | What is the principal point? | The image location at which the optical axis meets the image plane; represented by [cx,cy] in K. It need not equal the image centre. | HC1b pp.39–63
camera | core | What does skew in K model? | Non-orthogonality of image coordinate axes; in the usual convention s is K[0,1]. It is usually assumed zero for modern cameras. | HC1b pp.39,62–65
camera | core | How many free parameters does the usual general K have? | Five: fx, fy, skew, cx and cy, after choosing K[2,2]=1. Additional distortion parameters are not entries of this K. | HC1b pp.39–45
camera | core | What do camera extrinsics describe? | Camera orientation and position relative to a world frame, through a rigid coordinate transformation. | HC1b pp.35–45; S1
camera | core | What does Xc=R*Xw+t mean? | R and t map a world-coordinate point into camera coordinates. This is the world-to-camera convention, not its inverse. | HC1b pp.35–45
camera | core | What is the full homogeneous camera projection equation? | x is proportional to P*Xw_h, where P=K*[R|t] is 3 by 4 and Xw_h=[X,Y,Z,1]. Dehomogenize the resulting image vector. | HC1b pp.35–45; HC6b p.27
camera | core | What are the shapes of K, R, t and [R|t]? | K: 3x3; R: 3x3; t: 3x1; [R|t]: 3x4. Their product P=K*[R|t] is 3x4. | HC1b pp.35–45
camera | core | What is the camera centre C when Xc=R*Xw+t? | C=-R.T*t for a rotation matrix. Equivalently t=-R*C. Translation t is not generally the world-frame camera position. | HC1b pp.35–45; S1
camera | core | What constraints define a proper rotation matrix? | R.T*R=I and det(R)=+1. Its inverse is R.T; a proper 3D rotation has 3 degrees of freedom. | HC1b pp.41–45; S1
camera | core | Does the order of 3D rotations matter? | Generally yes: R1*R2 is not R2*R1. With column vectors, the rightmost matrix acts first. | HC1b pp.41–42
camera | core | Give the 2D counterclockwise rotation matrix in Cartesian x-right, y-up coordinates. | R(theta)=[[cos(theta),-sin(theta)],[sin(theta),cos(theta)]]. Displayed image rotation can look reversed when y points down. | HC4a pp.11–13; S1
camera | core | What is perspective division? | Divide the first two entries of a projected homogeneous image vector by its third entry to obtain pixel coordinates. | HC1b pp.24–45
camera | core | Can one image pixel determine unique depth? | No. Without further constraints it specifies a viewing ray; many depths along that ray produce the same pixel. | HC1a; HC1b pp.24–34
camera | core | What is orthographic projection? | Projection along parallel viewing rays; in its simplest form [X,Y,Z] maps to [X,Y], so image scale does not vary with Z. | HC2a p.23; Forsyth–Ponce photometric-stereo section
camera | core | How do perspective and orthographic models differ in their dependence on depth? | Perspective divides camera X and Y by Z, making scale depth-dependent. Orthographic projection ignores Z in its basic image-position mapping and uses parallel rays. | HC1b pp.24–26; HC2a p.23
camera | core | What does resizing both image axes by factor a do to K in a continuous-coordinate model? | Scale the first two rows by a: focal scales, skew and principal-point coordinates scale. Keep the last row [0,0,1] and extrinsics unchanged. | HC1b projection model; S1
camera | core | What does cropping an image at top-left offset (x0,y0) do to K? | cx becomes cx-x0 and cy becomes cy-y0. Focal scales and skew stay unchanged if there is no resize. | HC1b projection model; S1
camera | breadth | How are radial and tangential lens distortion different? | Radial distortion depends on distance from the image's distortion centre, usually near the principal point, causing barrel/pincushion effects. Tangential distortion relates to lens/sensor misalignment. | HC1b pp.46–53; HC6b pp.35–37
camera | core | Why undistort images before using an ideal homography or epipolar model? | Lens distortion is nonlinear and is not included in the simple pinhole K[R|t] model; correcting it makes the ideal geometric relations applicable. | HC1b pp.46–65; HC6b pp.35–37
camera | breadth | What is camera calibration? | Estimate camera parameters, such as intrinsics, distortion and/or extrinsics, from suitable known geometry and observations. | HC6b pp.26–47
camera | breadth | How many degrees of freedom has a general pinhole projection matrix? | Eleven up to scale: 5 intrinsic plus 6 rigid extrinsic parameters. Additional distortion is outside that count. | HC1b p.43
camera | breadth | What are vanishing points? | Image locations where projections of parallel scene lines meet, possibly outside the image or at infinity. They encode scene directions. | HC1b pp.28–31; HC3a pp.70–73
camera | breadth | What is a vanishing line or horizon for a scene plane? | The line containing vanishing points of directions lying in that plane. For a level ground plane it forms its geometric horizon. | HC1b pp.28–31; S1

# Colour and digital representation
colour | core | How are ordinary image arrays indexed in NumPy? | I[row,column]=I[y,x]; an RGB array usually has shape (H,W,3), with channels on the last axis. | HC1b pp.75–78; Lab0; Lab1
colour | breadth | What is sampling in digital image formation? | Measuring a continuous image at discrete spatial locations, usually a regular grid of sensor elements. | HC1b pp.69–78
colour | breadth | What is quantization in digital image formation? | Representing continuous measurement values with a finite set of discrete levels, such as integers 0..255. | HC1b pp.69–78
colour | core | How many intensity levels can an unsigned b-bit channel represent? | 2^b levels, normally 0 through 2^b-1. For 8 bits: 256 levels, not 255 levels. | HC1b pp.77–78; S3 4-bit examples
colour | core | What are usual normalized-float and uint8 image ranges? | Float images often use [0,1]; uint8 images use integers [0,255]. A float type alone does not guarantee normalization. | HC1b pp.77–78; Lab0; Lab1
colour | core | What colour-channel order does OpenCV commonly use for loaded colour images? | BGR, not RGB. Convert or reverse channels when an RGB-based display or calculation requires it. | HC1b p.87; Lab0; Lab1
colour | core | What is the average-method RGB grayscale value? | (R+G+B)/3. It treats all channels equally and is not a perceptual luminance weighting. | Lab1 Section 1
colour | core | What is the lightness-method RGB grayscale value? | (max(R,G,B)+min(R,G,B))/2. This matches the coding-prep example, not the channel average. | Lab1; Coding examples Q2
colour | core | What is a common weighted luminosity/luma grayscale approximation? | About 0.299*R + 0.587*G + 0.114*B for RGB channel values. This is a conventional weighted approximation, not a universal physical luminance formula. Use supplied coefficients and channel order. | Lab1 Section 1; Szeliski §2.3
colour | breadth | Why do RGB channels not uniquely specify a physical spectrum? | A sensor compresses the wavelength-dependent signal into three responses. Different spectra can give the same responses. | HC1b pp.80–92; Szeliski §2.3
colour | core | What determines measured RGB for an illuminated surface? | Source spectral power, material spectral reflectance and sensor channel sensitivities, combined over wavelength, plus geometry and camera response. | HC1b pp.67–71,80–86
colour | breadth | What does H mean in HSV? | Hue: the angular/cyclic colour type, such as red, green or blue. It is unstable or undefined when saturation is zero. | HC1b pp.88–91; Lab1
colour | breadth | What does S mean in HSV? | Saturation: colour purity relative to gray. S=0 is achromatic in the standard definition. | HC1b pp.88–91; Lab1
colour | breadth | What does V mean in HSV? | Value/brightness, commonly max(R,G,B) for normalized RGB. It is not the same quantity as HSL lightness. | HC1b pp.88–91; Lab1
colour | core | What assumption does Grey-World white balance make? | Under neutral illumination, the scene's average colour is gray, so average R, G and B responses should be equal. | Lab1; Coding examples Q3,Q4
colour | core | Write the Grey-World channel-scaling rule. | Compute spatial channel means m_c, choose a neutral target such as their mean m, then I'_c=I_c*m/m_c. Handle zero means; clip/quantize only after correction. | Lab1; Coding examples Q3,Q4
colour | core | What axes compute per-channel means for shape (H,W,3)? | axis=(0,1). With keepdims=True the result has shape (1,1,3), suitable for channel-wise broadcasting. | Coding examples Q3
colour | core | Why does I.mean(axis=(0,1,2)) break the intended Grey-World correction? | It collapses channels to the same overall mean. Dividing and multiplying by that scalar cancels instead of correcting channel imbalance. | Coding examples Q4
colour | core | When can Grey-World fail? | When the scene's average material colour is not gray, such as a strongly monochromatic scene, or when clipping and nonuniform illumination invalidate the model. | Lab1
colour | breadth | What assumption does White-Patch white balance make, as one example for Lab1's open-choice prompt? | A brightest or reference patch is assumed to reflect the illuminant neutrally; channel responses there estimate colour cast. This is one possible additional colour-constancy method, not a named required algorithm in the notebook. | Lab1 Q8 (chosen example); Forsyth–Ponce §2.2 brightest-patch assumption
colour | core | Why can unsigned-integer image subtraction be unsafe? | Unsigned arithmetic can wrap around on negative results. Convert to a suitable floating-point or signed type before difference/gradient computations. | Lab0; Lab2
colour | core | Why convert to float before weighted sums or intensity correction? | It avoids integer overflow and preserves fractional values during calculation. Convert back only after required scaling and clipping. | Lab0; Lab1; Lab2
colour | breadth | What is sensor dynamic range? | The range between the weakest usable signal and saturation, often limited by noise at the low end and clipping at the high end. | HC1b p.71; Szeliski §2.3
colour | breadth | What is the rolling-shutter problem? | Different image rows are exposed at different times, so scene or camera motion can distort the captured geometry. | HC1b p.71

# Reflection
reflectance | core | What is a surface normal? | A direction perpendicular to the local tangent plane, usually represented as a unit outward vector. Its components depend on the coordinate frame. | HC2a p.11
reflectance | core | What is albedo in the simplified diffuse model? | The material's diffuse reflectance factor, separated from lighting and orientation. Physical calibrated reflectance is normally in [0,1]. | HC2a pp.6,19–20
reflectance | core | What is irradiance? | Incoming radiant power per unit surface area, integrated over incoming directions. It describes illumination arriving at a surface. | HC2a pp.11–12; Szeliski §2.2
reflectance | core | What is radiance? | Directional radiant power per unit projected area per unit solid angle. The camera measures a response related to incoming scene radiance. | HC2a pp.11–20; Szeliski §2.2
reflectance | core | What is a directional light source? | A model with parallel rays and constant direction over the object, approximating an infinitely distant source. Strength can be absorbed into its vector magnitude. | HC2a pp.7–8,19
reflectance | core | What does Lambert's cosine law say for illumination? | Received irradiance is proportional to the positive cosine of the angle between the surface normal and direction toward the light. | HC2a pp.12–14
reflectance | core | Write the simplified calibrated Lambertian pixel-intensity model. | I=rho*J*max(n dot l,0), with unit n and l. Strength J and gain may instead be encoded in the light vector and effective diffuse coefficient. | HC2a pp.12–20; Lab1
reflectance | core | In Lambertian lighting, what happens as the light–normal angle approaches 90 degrees? | The diffuse contribution approaches zero because cos(angle) approaches zero. Shadows and other effects may require more than this local model. | HC2a pp.12–20
reflectance | core | Is ideal Lambertian radiance view-dependent? | No, for fixed material, illumination and normal. Projected surface area and visibility can still change with view. | HC2a pp.13–18
reflectance | core | How do ideal diffuse and ideal specular reflection differ? | Diffuse reflection has view-independent radiance; ideal specular reflection directs light along the mirror-reflection direction. | HC2a pp.9–10,25–27
reflectance | breadth | What is the distinction between body and surface reflection? | Body scattering often produces diffuse matte appearance; surface reflection produces specular/glossy highlights. Real materials can have both. | HC2a p.26
reflectance | core | What is a BRDF? | Bidirectional Reflectance Distribution Function: relates incoming irradiance from a direction to outgoing radiance in another direction at a surface point. | HC2a pp.28–32
reflectance | breadth | Why is the general BRDF a four-dimensional angular function? | Incoming direction needs two angles and outgoing direction needs two more, relative to the surface frame. | HC2a pp.29–30
reflectance | breadth | What is Helmholtz reciprocity for an ordinary reciprocal BRDF? | Interchanging incoming and outgoing directions leaves the BRDF value unchanged. It is a material reciprocity property, not equality of all image intensities. | HC2a p.30
reflectance | breadth | Why does a general BRDF complicate normal estimation? | The surface normal determines the local angular frame, so it enters both the cosine term and the BRDF's directional arguments. The resulting inverse problem is generally nonlinear. | HC2a pp.28–32
reflectance | core | What are the three terms in the Phong illumination model? | Ambient, diffuse and specular contributions, added together. | HC2a p.33
reflectance | core | Write the simplified Phong diffuse and specular terms. | Diffuse: kd*max(n dot l,0). Specular: ks*max(v dot r,0)^s, where r is the ideal reflection direction and s is shininess. Add an ambient term if specified. | HC2a p.33
reflectance | core | What happens when the Phong shininess exponent increases? | The highlight becomes narrower and sharper, for the same coefficient and directions. | HC2a p.33
reflectance | core | How can a mirror sphere help calibrate light direction? | Its known geometry gives the normal at the highlight, and the known viewing direction plus the law of reflection determines the light direction. | HC2a p.27; Lab1
reflectance | breadth | With n and v pointing outward/toward the viewer, what is the reflected light-direction relation at a mirror highlight? | l=2*(n dot v)*n-v for unit vectors under this outward-direction convention. Other sign conventions require corresponding changes. | HC2a p.27; Lab1
reflectance | core | What is intrinsic image decomposition? | Separate image appearance into material/albedo and illumination/shading, commonly using I=A*S elementwise in a simplified model. | HC2a pp.47–48; Lab1
reflectance | core | How do you recolour an object while keeping estimated shading? | Modify its albedo layer inside the object mask, keep shading fixed, and multiply the layers to reconstruct the image. | Lab1; Coding examples Q6
reflectance | core | Why is shape from one shaded image ambiguous? | Intensity combines unknown material, lighting and geometry. Even with known albedo and light, one local intensity gives insufficient information for a unique normal. | HC2a pp.4–5,21–23
reflectance | breadth | What simplifying assumptions are listed for classical shape from shading? | Diffuse material with constant albedo, known distant illumination and an orthographic/distant camera, with additional constraints needed to recover shape. | HC2a pp.21–23

# Photometric stereo
photometric | core | What observations does photometric stereo use? | Multiple images of the same fixed scene from the same viewpoint under different lighting conditions. | HC2a pp.6,34–45; Lab1
photometric | core | What does calibrated photometric stereo estimate at each pixel? | An effective albedo-scaled normal g, then albedo from its magnitude and the unit normal from its direction. A height map requires further integration. | HC2a pp.34–43
photometric | core | Write the per-pixel linear photometric-stereo system with lights as rows. | i=L*g, where L has shape (number_of_lights,3), i has one intensity per image, and g=rho*n when strength/gain are calibrated into L. | HC2a pp.34–39; Lab1
photometric | core | Why can photometric-stereo matrix orientation differ between sources? | Some stack light vectors as rows and use i=L*g; others use columns and pixels as rows, giving I=G*L. Check shapes before using a formula. | HC2a pp.34–39; Lab1; SOURCE_NOTES
photometric | core | What is the theoretical minimum number of independent calibrated lights for unconstrained diffuse photometric stereo? | Three, with linearly independent light vectors and valid unshadowed linear measurements. Three images alone do not guarantee independence. | HC2a pp.34–36; Lab1
photometric | core | When is a 3 by 3 lighting matrix invertible? | When its light vectors span 3D, so rank(L)=3. Coplanar vectors through the origin do not meet this condition. | HC2a p.36
photometric | core | What is the least-squares solution for full-column-rank L? | g=(L.T*L)^(-1)*L.T*i in exact algebra. In computation use a stable least-squares routine rather than explicitly inverting the normal equations. | HC2a p.39; Coding examples Q1,Q5
photometric | core | What does np.linalg.lstsq(L,i,rcond=None)[0] return? | A vector minimizing ||L*g-i||_2^2. It is not gradient descent and does not require a learning rate. A full-rank overdetermined system need not fit exactly. | Coding examples Q1,Q5
photometric | core | How is albedo recovered from a solved g? | rho=||g||_2, under the calibrated effective-reflectance convention. Unknown light strength or gain changes its absolute scale. | HC2a pp.35–39; Lab1
photometric | core | How is the unit surface normal recovered from g? | n=g/||g||_2, provided ||g|| is nonzero. Mask zero or invalid estimates rather than claiming a direction. | HC2a pp.35–39; Lab1
photometric | core | What does adding more independent photometric-stereo images provide? | An overdetermined fit that can reduce noise sensitivity and expose residual/model errors. It does not automatically eliminate systematic shadows or non-Lambertian effects. | HC2a pp.39–45; Lab1
photometric | core | What are major violations of the basic photometric-stereo model? | Shadows, specular highlights, interreflections, unknown/incorrect light calibration, nonlinear camera response, motion and changing viewpoint. | HC2a pp.19–20,45; Lab1
photometric | core | How can shadows affect a least-squares photometric-stereo fit? | A dark observation may reflect occlusion of the light rather than the linear dot product, so treating it as an ordinary equation can bias the normal. | Lab1 shadow trick; HC2a p.45
photometric | core | What does weighted least squares do in photometric stereo? | Reduce the influence of unreliable observations by minimizing a specified weighted residual sum. The chosen weighting rule is a heuristic/model choice, not a universal shadow solution. | Lab1
photometric | core | If rows and intensities are both multiplied by w_j, what residual weight is minimized? | sum_j w_j^2*(L_j*g-i_j)^2. Multiplying the rows by w_j gives squared weights, not w_j alone. | Lab1 weighted estimation; prerequisite linear algebra
photometric | core | Can dependent lighting be repaired merely by collecting more images with the same dependent directions? | No. Additional observations can improve noise estimates but cannot recover directions in the lighting matrix's nullspace. | HC2a pp.36–39
photometric | core | What happens if every true light strength is a common factor a larger than the strengths used in the fit? | The fitted g and albedo scale by a; the normalized normal stays unchanged for a>0 in the ideal model. | HC2a pp.19–20,34–39
photometric | core | For a graph z(x,y), what upward normal convention is used in this pack? | n is proportional to [-dz/dx,-dz/dy,1]. Thus p=-nx/nz and q=-ny/nz. Lab1 prints positive ratios instead; the local sources do not resolve that discrepancy. Follow the explicitly declared convention. | HC2a p.42; Lab1; SOURCE_NOTES
photometric | core | What is the integrability condition for height derivatives p and q? | dp/dy=dq/dx for a sufficiently smooth height field on a simply connected region. A violation means the estimated field need not be path-independent. | HC2a pp.42–43; Lab1
photometric | core | How does simple discrete normal integration recover height? | Starting from an anchor, accumulate p along x steps and q along y steps, using the specified spacing and path. Noise can accumulate. | HC2a pp.41–43; Lab1
photometric | core | Why can different integration paths give different reconstructed heights? | The estimated gradients can be non-integrable due to noise, calibration errors or model violations. Mixed-derivative differences reveal this inconsistency. | Lab1; HC2a pp.42–43
photometric | core | What ambiguity remains after integrating a valid gradient field without a height anchor? | An additive height constant. Relative height changes are constrained; absolute height is not. | HC2a p.43; Lab1; Forsyth–Ponce §2.2
photometric | core | Why is dividing by nz dangerous when nz is near zero? | The surface approaches a vertical tangent relative to the height-field model, so estimated slopes can become arbitrarily large and unstable. | HC2a p.42; Lab1
photometric | core | Why can solving colour channels separately produce invalid normals in a zero channel? | A channel with zero reflectance can have zero observations and g=0, so its normal cannot be normalized. Use a valid shared-geometry strategy or masking. | Lab1 colour photometric stereo
photometric | breadth | How can a global height least-squares system improve on one-path integration? | Solve neighbouring height differences to best fit all measured slopes, with a height anchor, distributing inconsistent/noisy errors rather than accumulating them along one path. | HC2a p.43
photometric | breadth | Who is associated with classical photometric stereo? | Robert Woodham. The essential idea is fixed-view images under different illumination, unlike geometric stereo with different viewpoints. | HC2a p.41; Forsyth–Ponce notes

# Point processing and histograms
histogram | core | What is an image intensity histogram? | Counts of pixels in each intensity level or bin. It summarizes the intensity distribution, not the spatial arrangement. | HC2a pp.51–57
histogram | core | How is a normalized histogram formed? | Divide bin counts by the total number of counted pixels. Its entries sum to 1 if all pixels are included. | HC2a p.54
histogram | core | What is the cumulative distribution function of an image histogram? | CDF(r)=sum of normalized counts for levels <=r. It is nondecreasing and reaches 1 at the highest included level. | HC2a p.54
histogram | breadth | What is the effect of choosing too many or too few histogram bins? | Too many can give sparse/noisy estimates; too few merge distinct intensities or colours and lose detail. | HC2a pp.52–53
histogram | core | What spatial information does an ordinary histogram lose? | Pixel positions, arrangement, boundaries and connectivity. Different layouts can have identical histograms. | HC2a pp.51–56
histogram | core | What information do separate RGB channel histograms lose compared with a joint histogram? | Cross-channel associations: which R,G,B values occur together. Both ordinary forms still lose spatial layout. | HC2a pp.55–56
histogram | core | What is a point operator? | An operation whose output at a pixel depends on that pixel's value, not its neighbours. Its mapping can be fixed or estimated from global statistics. | HC2a pp.58–63
histogram | core | Write a linear contrast-stretch mapping from [a,b] to [c,d]. | s=c+(r-a)*(d-c)/(b-a), for b>a. Clip outside the input range if the stated rule requires it. | HC2a pp.59–60; S1
histogram | core | What is the power-law gamma mapping used in the slides? | s=c*r^gamma. For normalized r in (0,1), gamma<1 brightens and gamma>1 darkens; gamma=1 is linear scaling. | HC2a pp.61–62; S1
histogram | core | What is the central idea of histogram equalization? | Map intensity through a scaled cumulative histogram/CDF to spread the output distribution across the available range. | HC2a p.63; S1
histogram | core | Does discrete histogram equalization guarantee a perfectly flat histogram? | No. Finite counts, quantization and merging levels can prevent exact uniformity. Equalization cannot split identical input levels using a single deterministic point mapping. | HC2a p.63; S1
histogram | core | Why must numerical histogram-equalization questions specify a mapping convention? | Choices such as CDF_min subtraction, number of levels and rounding/flooring can produce different output levels. Follow the stated rule. | HC2a p.63; S1
histogram | breadth | Why can separately equalizing R, G and B alter image colours? | The three different mappings can change channel ratios, and therefore hue/chromaticity. An intensity-channel approach can limit this, but must use a suitable colour space and range handling. | HC2a histograms; Lab1 colour spaces
histogram | breadth | Why can global contrast enhancement be undesirable? | It can amplify noise, change intended appearance or fail to improve small regions whose local statistics differ from the overall image. | HC2a pp.57–63; Szeliski §3.1

# Morphology and region processing
morphology | core | What is 4-connectivity in a 2D grid? | Neighbours share an edge: up, down, left and right. Diagonal contact alone does not connect foreground pixels. | HC2b p.10; S2
morphology | core | What is 8-connectivity in a 2D grid? | Include the four edge neighbours and four diagonal neighbours. Diagonal contact can connect foreground pixels. | HC2b p.10; S2
morphology | core | What is a connected foreground component? | A maximal set of foreground pixels joined by foreground paths under the specified neighbourhood rule. | HC2b pp.7–10
morphology | core | Does connected-component count necessarily equal physical object count? | No. Touching objects can merge; fragmented objects can split. Connectivity is a property of the binary representation. | HC2b pp.5–12,38
morphology | core | What does the first pass of two-pass connected-component labelling do? | Scan foreground pixels, assign provisional labels using already-seen neighbours, and record equivalences where labelled branches meet. | HC2b p.11
morphology | core | What does the second pass of connected-component labelling do? | Resolve label equivalences and relabel pixels consistently so each connected region has one final label. | HC2b p.12
morphology | core | What is a structuring element? | A spatial support mask with a defined origin, specifying which relative positions participate in a morphological operation. | HC2b p.14
morphology | core | For a symmetric binary structuring element, how is dilation computed locally? | The output is 1 if any active support position sees foreground; equivalently the maximum over active positions. | HC2b pp.15–24; S2
morphology | core | For a binary structuring element, how is erosion computed locally? | The output is 1 only if every active support position sees foreground; equivalently the minimum over active positions. | HC2b pp.27–36; S2
morphology | core | Are zero entries of a structuring-element mask required zeros in the image? | No. They are inactive positions for ordinary dilation/erosion. Only active support positions impose tests. | HC2b pp.14–29; S2
morphology | core | What effects can binary dilation have? | Grow foreground, join nearby components and reduce sufficiently small background gaps/holes, depending on element shape and size. | HC2b pp.15–26
morphology | core | What effects can binary erosion have? | Shrink foreground, remove small protrusions/bridges, split touching regions or eliminate small components. | HC2b pp.27–38
morphology | core | What is opening? | Erosion followed by dilation with the same structuring element: open(B)=(B eroded by K) dilated by K. | HC2b pp.39–42; S2
morphology | core | What is closing? | Dilation followed by erosion with the same structuring element: close(B)=(B dilated by K) eroded by K. | HC2b pp.43–45; S2
morphology | core | What is opening mainly used to remove? | Small foreground structures or narrow foreground connections that the structuring element cannot fit inside. | HC2b pp.39–42
morphology | core | What is closing mainly used to remove? | Small background gaps, cracks or holes relative to the structuring element; it can join nearby foreground regions. | HC2b pp.43–45
morphology | core | Is erosion followed by dilation generally an exact inverse operation? | No. Opening can permanently remove small foreground structures. Closing also need not restore the original image. | HC2b pp.39–45; S2
morphology | breadth | Are ideal opening and closing idempotent? | Yes under their consistent mathematical definitions: applying the same opening or closing again changes nothing. Finite-image boundary handling can alter practical behaviour. | HC2b pp.39–45; Szeliski §3.3
morphology | core | Why must a morphology question specify the image boundary rule? | Out-of-image samples can change erosion/dilation and counts near borders. Zero padding, reflection and other rules are not equivalent. | S2; HC2b morphology
morphology | core | What happens to the border of an all-ones image under 3x3 erosion with outside values zero? | Border pixels whose support leaves the image become zero; only the interior with complete foreground support survives. | S2; HC2b pp.27–38
morphology | core | How can morphology support counting touching objects? | Threshold to foreground, separate suitable narrow connections with erosion/opening, then label and count connected regions. This can damage true small objects. | HC2b pp.38–42
morphology | breadth | What is the binary component area? | The number of foreground pixels in the component; multiply by physical pixel area only when such calibration is given. | HC2b p.9
morphology | breadth | How is a binary component centroid calculated? | Mean the x and y coordinates of its foreground pixels, or equivalently divide first spatial moments by area. | HC2b p.9; prerequisite aggregation
morphology | breadth | How can dilation produce an outer foreground boundary? | Compute dilated(B)-B with the stated element and boundary rule. Erosion can similarly give an inner boundary B-eroded(B). | HC2b p.26

filtering | core | What makes an image operator linear? | Additivity and homogeneity: H(aI+bJ)=aH(I)+bH(J). Fixed weighted sums satisfy this; median, max and thresholding generally do not. | HC2b pp.47–50
filtering | core | Write 2D discrete mathematical convolution with offsets centred at the kernel origin. | (I*K)[y,x]=sum over offsets (dy,dx) K[dy,dx]*I[y-dy,x-dx]. The sign reversal is essential. | HC2b pp.78–80
filtering | core | Write a centred sliding cross-correlation operation. | C[y,x]=sum over offsets (dy,dx) K[dy,dx]*I[y+dy,x+dx]. The kernel is not flipped. | HC2b pp.78–80
filtering | core | When do correlation and convolution with a kernel give the same values? | When the kernel is symmetric under reversal of both spatial axes, with compatible alignment and boundary rules. | HC2b pp.78–80
filtering | core | What operation is commonly called convolution in CNN libraries? | Sliding cross-correlation with learned kernels, usually without flipping them. It is a naming convention; compute using the specified forward rule. | HC2b p.50; HC5b pp.25–33
filtering | core | What is a normalized box filter? | Equal weights over a window, divided by the number of positions. It replaces each pixel with a local mean under the boundary rule. | HC2b pp.51–72; S2
filtering | core | Why normalize a smoothing kernel to sum to one? | To preserve the intensity of constant regions where the full support has valid constant samples. Zero-padded boundaries can still darken. | HC2b pp.51–87; Lab2
filtering | core | What is the isotropic 2D Gaussian kernel formula? | G(x,y)=1/(2*pi*sigma^2)*exp(-(x^2+y^2)/(2*sigma^2)) in the continuous form. A sampled truncated kernel is often renormalized. | HC2b pp.82–87; S2
filtering | core | What does increasing Gaussian sigma do? | Increase spatial smoothing scale, suppress more fine detail/noise and broaden features. | HC2b pp.82–85
filtering | core | What is Gaussian separability? | G(x,y)=g(x)*g(y). A 2D Gaussian filter can be applied as two 1D filters along the two axes. | HC2b pp.86–87; S2; Lab2
filtering | core | What is the per-pixel cost advantage of a separable k by k kernel? | Roughly 2*k multiplications instead of k^2, excluding implementation overhead. The benefit requires a separable kernel. | HC2b pp.86–87; S2; Lab2
filtering | core | How do standard deviations combine when convolving two ideal Gaussians? | sigma_total=sqrt(sigma1^2+sigma2^2), because variances add. This is idealized before finite sampling/truncation. | HC2b p.85; S2
filtering | core | What extra Gaussian sigma is needed to reach sigma_target from existing sigma_current? | sqrt(sigma_target^2-sigma_current^2), provided target>=current and the ideal Gaussian model applies. | HC2b p.85; S2
filtering | core | What is a low-pass image filter? | Passes relatively slowly varying components and suppresses high spatial frequencies; common smoothing filters are low-pass. | HC2b pp.93–105
filtering | core | What is a high-pass image filter? | Suppresses slowly varying components and emphasizes rapid intensity changes such as fine texture or edges. | HC2b pp.93–105
filtering | core | What sum should an ordinary discrete derivative kernel have? | Zero, so a constant region gives zero derivative when the boundary rule does not introduce changes. | HC2b pp.74–80; HC3a; Lab2
filtering | core | What is the image gradient? | The vector [Ix,Iy] of spatial intensity derivatives, pointing toward the fastest intensity increase. | HC3a; S2; Lab2
filtering | core | What are gradient magnitude and orientation? | Magnitude=sqrt(Ix^2+Iy^2); orientation=atan2(Iy,Ix), using the stated coordinate axes. | HC3a pp.20–57; S2
filtering | core | How is gradient direction related to the edge direction? | The gradient is approximately perpendicular to the edge tangent. An x derivative detects intensity change across x, often at a vertical edge. | HC2b pp.74–76; HC3a
filtering | core | Why smooth before taking image derivatives? | Differentiation amplifies high-frequency noise. Smoothing limits noise while setting the spatial analysis scale. | HC3a; S2; Lab2
filtering | core | What is the derivative-of-convolution identity used for edge detection? | d(I*G)/dx=I*(dG/dx) under appropriate regularity/boundary assumptions. Smooth-and-differentiate can be combined in one derivative-of-Gaussian filter. | HC3a; S2; Lab2
filtering | core | What is a Sobel derivative kernel in one common cross-correlation x convention? | [[-1,0,1],[-2,0,2],[-1,0,1]], combining x differencing with y smoothing. Some slides/libraries use the opposite sign; convolution flips it. | HC2b pp.75–80; Lab2; SOURCE_NOTES
filtering | core | What is a median filter? | Replace a pixel by the median of neighbourhood values under a specified window/boundary rule. It is nonlinear. | HC2b pp.89–91; S2; Lab2
filtering | core | Why can a median filter suppress sparse salt-and-pepper noise? | The middle-ranked value is less affected by isolated extreme outliers than an arithmetic mean, while preserving some sharp transitions. | HC2b pp.89–91; S2
filtering | core | Does a median filter guarantee preservation of every edge and thin structure? | No. Its window can remove thin structures or shift details; success depends on noise density and feature size. | HC2b pp.89–91; Lab2
filtering | core | What do valid, same and full convolution output modes mean at a high level? | Valid: only full-overlap positions. Same: output aligned to an input-sized grid under a stated boundary rule. Full: all partial-overlap positions. Check even-kernel alignment. | Lab2; S2
filtering | core | What is the full linear-convolution output size for lengths N and M? | N+M-1. In 2D use H+Kh-1 by W+Kw-1. | HC2b convolution; Lab2; Szeliski §3.2
filtering | core | What is aliasing during image downsampling? | Frequencies too high for the new grid become indistinguishable from lower-frequency patterns. Low-pass filtering before subsampling can reduce it. | HC2b pp.93–105; HC5b p.39
filtering | breadth | What is a Gabor filter? | A Gaussian envelope multiplied by a sinusoidal carrier. It analyzes local orientation and spatial frequency, often in a filter bank. | HC2b pp.106–107; Lab2
filtering | breadth | Why do padding choices affect convolution outputs? | The effective neighbourhood values near edges differ. Zero, reflected, replicated and periodic extensions can give different boundary responses. | S2; Lab2

# Fourier analysis
fourier | core | What does the Fourier transform represent? | A signal or image as weighted sinusoidal/complex-exponential components indexed by spatial frequency, with magnitude and phase. | HC2b pp.93–104
fourier | core | What does low spatial frequency describe? | Slowly varying intensity structure and broad patterns. Zero frequency is the constant/DC component. | HC2b pp.93–105
fourier | core | What does high spatial frequency describe? | Rapid changes over short spatial distances, such as fine texture and sharp transitions. Noise can also have high-frequency content. | HC2b pp.93–105
fourier | core | What is Euler's formula? | exp(i*theta)=cos(theta)+i*sin(theta); therefore exp(-i*theta)=cos(theta)-i*sin(theta). | HC2b p.99; SOURCE_NOTES
fourier | core | How are sinusoid frequency u, angular frequency omega and period T related? | omega=2*pi*u and T=1/u for positive nonzero u. Use consistent spatial/time units. | HC2b pp.96–99
fourier | core | For A*exp(i*(omega*x+phi)), what do A, omega and phi mean? | A is magnitude/radius, omega is angular rate, and phi is the starting phase angle. Real and imaginary parts are cosine and sine components. | HC2b pp.97–100
fourier | core | How are real and imaginary components recovered from magnitude A and phase phi? | real=A*cos(phi), imaginary=A*sin(phi). | HC2b pp.97–100
fourier | core | How is the magnitude of a+ib computed? | sqrt(a^2+b^2), equivalently abs(a+1j*b). Magnitude squared is a^2+b^2, not the same quantity. | HC2b p.100
fourier | core | How is the phase of a+ib computed in NumPy? | np.arctan2(b,a): imaginary first, real second. It retains the correct quadrant. | HC2b p.100; SOURCE_NOTES
fourier | core | Why use atan2 rather than atan(b/a) for phase? | atan2 handles quadrants and a=0 correctly. A plain ratio loses quadrant information and can divide by zero. Phase is undefined at zero magnitude. | HC2b pp.97–100; Lab2
fourier | core | Write the unnormalized N-point forward DFT convention used in the practice questions. | F[k]=sum(n=0..N-1) f[n]*exp(-2*pi*i*k*n/N). The inverse has a positive exponent and a 1/N factor. | HC2b pp.102–103; SOURCE_NOTES
fourier | core | What is F[0] in an unnormalized DFT? | The sum of all samples. Divide by sample count to obtain the mean. Other normalization conventions scale this value. | HC2b pp.99–103
fourier | core | Why can a real-valued image have a complex-valued Fourier transform? | Complex coefficients encode sinusoid magnitude and phase through two components. Real input does not mean every phase is zero. | HC2b pp.97–104
fourier | core | What symmetry does the DFT of a real signal have? | F[-k]=conjugate(F[k]) with indices modulo N; in images use the corresponding opposite frequency pair on both axes. | HC2b pp.99–104; Szeliski §3.4
fourier | core | State the convolution theorem under a compatible normalization. | Fourier transform of spatial convolution is pointwise multiplication of spectra. Spatial multiplication corresponds to frequency convolution, with convention-dependent scale factors. | HC2b p.101; Szeliski §3.4
fourier | core | What boundary assumption does an unpadded DFT convolution make? | Periodic/circular boundaries. Products of finite DFTs yield circular convolution, not automatically full linear convolution. | HC2b pp.101–103; Szeliski §3.4
fourier | core | How much zero padding is needed for 1D DFT-based full linear convolution? | At least N+M-1 transform samples for signals of lengths N and M. In 2D pad each axis to the corresponding summed size minus one. | HC2b pp.101–103; Szeliski §3.4
fourier | core | What does a spatial translation do to the Fourier spectrum in the ideal/circular case? | Multiply by a unit-magnitude phase factor exp(-i*omega*shift). Magnitude stays unchanged, while phase changes with frequency. | HC2b Fourier; Szeliski §3.4
fourier | core | Does Fourier magnitude alone determine a general image? | No. Phase contains essential spatial arrangement information; different images can share magnitudes. | HC2b p.104; Szeliski §3.4
fourier | breadth | What does fftshift do to a frequency-array visualization? | Reorders entries to put zero frequency near the centre. It does not itself filter or change coefficient values. | HC2b p.104; Lab2
fourier | breadth | Why plot log magnitude rather than raw Fourier magnitude? | Large dynamic range can hide weaker components. A transform such as log(1+abs(F)) makes them visible without defining a new frequency spectrum. | HC2b p.104; Lab2
fourier | core | Does discarding the imaginary part of each Fourier coefficient preserve phase information? | No. It generally changes the signal and can destroy the representation of translated/asymmetric structure. | HC2b pp.97–104

features | core | Why does edge detection alone not identify complete object boundaries? | Edges can be fragmented, noisy or caused by texture/shadows; their membership in objects or lines is not automatically known. | HC3a pp.69–74

features | core | How does Gaussian scale influence detected edges? | Larger sigma reduces noise and fine detail but can merge or weaken closely spaced edges. Small sigma is more sensitive to fine structure and noise. | HC3a p.66

# Fitting and RANSAC
fitting | core | What does ordinary least squares minimize? | The sum of squared residuals under the chosen observation/model representation. For y=mx+b, these are vertical y residuals. | HC3a pp.76–79
fitting | core | Write the full-column-rank OLS solution for A*p=y. | p=(A.T*A)^(-1)*A.T*y. Numerically prefer a least-squares solver over forming an explicit inverse. | HC3a p.77; Coding examples Q1,Q5
fitting | core | Why can ordinary least squares be highly sensitive to outliers? | Squaring residuals gives extreme errors large influence, so a few outliers can substantially move the fitted model. | HC3a pp.79–88
fitting | core | What does total least squares change in 2D line fitting? | Use perpendicular distances and a symmetric line representation instead of choosing only vertical residuals in y=mx+b. | HC3a pp.78–82
fitting | core | What constant estimate minimizes summed squared scalar errors? | The arithmetic mean. Large outliers can strongly affect it. | HC3a pp.83–85
fitting | core | What constant estimate minimizes summed absolute scalar errors? | A median. For an even number of values, any value between the two central sorted values minimizes the absolute-error objective; their mean is a conventional median. | HC3a pp.83–85
fitting | breadth | What does Huber loss do at a high level? | Behaves quadratically for small residuals and linearly for large residuals, controlled by a transition parameter. It reduces outlier influence compared with pure squared loss. | HC3a p.86
fitting | core | What does RANSAC stand for? | RANdom SAmple Consensus. It estimates a model from small random subsets and evaluates their agreement with the full data. | HC3a p.91
fitting | breadth | Who introduced RANSAC? | Martin Fischler and Robert Bolles, 1981. | HC3a pp.91,103
fitting | core | What are the main RANSAC steps? | Sample a nondegenerate minimal subset; fit a candidate; compute residuals for all data; classify/count inliers; retain the best consensus; finally refit using its inliers. | HC3a pp.91–101; HC4a pp.45–48
fitting | core | What is an inlier in RANSAC? | A datum whose residual against the candidate model satisfies the stated threshold and residual definition. It is not necessarily a truly correct correspondence. | HC3a; HC4a p.48
fitting | core | Why reject a degenerate RANSAC sample? | Even if its points are correct, its geometry may not uniquely determine the requested model, such as collinear affine control points. | HC4a pp.47–55; Lab4
fitting | core | What are minimal sample sizes for a 2D line, circle, affine transform and homography? | Line: 2 distinct points. Circle: 3 noncollinear points. Affine: 3 noncollinear point pairs. Homography: 4 point pairs in general position. | HC3a pp.92–97; HC4a pp.47–50; Lab4
fitting | core | What is the all-inlier probability for an independent RANSAC sample of size s? | w^s, where w is the inlier fraction. Nondegeneracy is a separate requirement. | HC3a pp.104–105
fitting | core | What is the probability of at least one all-inlier sample after N independent RANSAC iterations? | p=1-(1-w^s)^N under the independent-sampling approximation. | HC3a pp.104–105
fitting | core | Give the minimum RANSAC iteration formula for desired success p. | N=ceil(log(1-p)/log(1-w^s)), for 0<w^s<1. Use the model's minimal sample size and round upwards. | HC3a pp.104–105
fitting | core | What increases the required RANSAC iteration count? | Lower inlier fraction, larger minimal sample size or higher desired success probability. | HC3a pp.104–109
fitting | core | Does RANSAC fail in principle whenever the inlier fraction is below 50%? | No. It can work with a minority of inliers, but may need a large iteration budget and careful model/threshold choice. | HC3a pp.104–109
fitting | core | Why refit the final RANSAC model on all selected inliers? | A minimal sample is noisy. Refitting uses more compatible data to improve precision after robust selection. | HC3a p.101; HC4a pp.46–48
fitting | core | Why does increasing the inlier threshold not necessarily improve RANSAC accuracy? | It can include erroneous correspondences and bias the refit. Thresholds must match the residual's units and expected noise. | HC4a p.48; HC3a p.109
fitting | breadth | Why is fitting several models harder than one basic RANSAC fit? | One consensus can dominate. Multiple structures need extra selection or repeated/multi-model procedures; one fit does not assign all scene lines. | HC3a p.109

# Optical flow
flow | core | What is optical flow? | The apparent 2D displacement or velocity field of image structure between frames, with units determined by frame/time spacing. | HC3b pp.8–38
flow | core | How does optical flow differ from 3D scene motion? | It is an image-plane apparent motion field, affected by projection and appearance. It is not directly a metric 3D velocity and can reflect lighting changes. | HC3b pp.8–38
flow | core | State brightness constancy for displacement (u,v) across one time step. | I(x+u,y+v,t+1)=I(x,y,t). It assumes the tracked appearance remains constant, apart from motion. | HC3b p.39
flow | core | Derive the local optical-flow constraint at a high level. | First-order Taylor-expand brightness constancy for small motion; cancel I(x,y,t), giving Ix*u+Iy*v+It=0. | HC3b p.40; S3
flow | core | Why does one optical-flow constraint not determine both u and v? | It gives one scalar equation for two unknown components. Only the gradient-normal motion component is constrained. | HC3b pp.40–47
flow | core | What is the aperture problem? | Locally observing a straight edge cannot reveal motion along the edge; different tangential motions can produce the same local change. | HC3b pp.41–47
flow | core | What extra assumption does local Lucas–Kanade make? | The pixels in a chosen neighbourhood share one approximately constant flow vector. Stack their constraints to estimate it. | HC3b pp.42–47; Lab3
flow | core | Write the Lucas–Kanade least-squares system and solution convention. | Rows of A are [Ix,Iy], b=-It; solve A*[u,v]=b in least squares. For full rank, flow=(A.T*A)^(-1)*A.T*b. | HC3b p.47; S3; Lab3
flow | core | When is a local Lucas–Kanade flow well constrained? | A has rank 2 and its structure tensor A.T*A has two sufficiently large eigenvalues with manageable condition number. | S3; Lab3
flow | core | What happens to Lucas–Kanade in a flat patch? | Spatial derivatives are near zero, so there is little information to determine either displacement component; estimates become unstable or unobservable. | HC3b p.47; Lab3
flow | core | What happens to Lucas–Kanade on a straight edge? | Gradient directions are nearly parallel; tangential motion is poorly constrained because the system is close to rank 1. | HC3b pp.41–47; S3
flow | core | Why can corners be better flow features than edges? | Independent local gradient directions constrain both u and v, matching the corner/structure-tensor criterion. | S3; Lab3
flow | core | What are key assumptions/conditions behind the basic Lucas–Kanade estimate? | Brightness constancy, small displacement for linearization, common local motion, sufficient texture and valid visible correspondence. | HC3b pp.39–47
flow | core | What violates brightness constancy? | Lighting changes, shadows, specular changes, nonlinear response changes or occlusion can change intensity without the assumed motion correspondence. | HC3b pp.57–58; Lab3
flow | core | Why can a large Lucas–Kanade window hurt near a motion boundary? | It combines pixels with different motion vectors, violating the shared-flow assumption even while adding more equations. | HC3b pp.42–47
flow | core | Why use a coarse-to-fine image pyramid for optical flow? | Large full-resolution motion becomes smaller in coarse pixels, making local linearization more plausible. Upsample, rescale, warp and refine. | HC3b pp.48–54
flow | core | How must flow vectors scale when going from half resolution to full resolution? | Multiply displacement components by 2 after spatial upsampling. Vector units depend on pixel size. | HC3b pp.52–54; Lab3
flow | core | What is warping's role in iterative flow estimation? | Use the current displacement to align frames, then estimate the smaller residual motion from the remaining mismatch. | HC3b pp.53–56
flow | core | What limitation can a coarse-to-fine flow pyramid have? | Small/thin structures can disappear at coarse levels, or an incorrect coarse match can trap finer refinement. It does not guarantee recovery of all large motion. | HC3b pp.52–58
flow | core | Why is occlusion a fundamental problem for flow correspondence? | A pixel's scene point may not be visible in the other frame, so its assumed matching observation does not exist. | HC3b pp.45–58
flow | breadth | How do sparse and dense optical flow differ? | Sparse flow estimates displacements at selected features; dense flow estimates a vector for most or all image pixels. | HC3b; Lab3
flow | breadth | How does Horn–Schunck differ from local Lucas–Kanade at a high level? | Horn–Schunck uses a global smoothness regularizer to encourage a dense smooth field; Lucas–Kanade uses local common-flow windows. Advanced Horn–Schunck details are optional in the lab. | HC3b pp.57–59; Lab3 optional material
flow | breadth | Give uses of optical flow beyond tracking. | Video stabilization, interpolation, motion analysis, denoising/super-resolution, compression and photometric consistency for learning. | HC3b pp.62–70

tracking | core | What is template-based tracking? | Search for the region in a new frame that best matches a fixed appearance template, often near the previous location. | HC3b pp.75–87
tracking | core | What is SSD in template matching? | Sum of squared differences: SSD(y)=sum_x (I(x+y)-T(x))^2. Select the displacement with smallest cost. | HC3b p.79
tracking | core | What is unnormalized cross-correlation in template matching? | CC(y)=sum_x I(x+y)*T(x). Select the largest response, but raw correlation can favour brightness/energy changes. | HC3b pp.79–87
tracking | core | Is raw SSD invariant to additive brightness change? | No. A constant offset changes the squared differences even for a geometrically correct match. Mean-normalized comparisons can reduce this sensitivity. | HC3b pp.75–87; Szeliski §9.1
tracking | breadth | What makes zero-mean normalized cross-correlation relatively robust to brightness offset and positive gain? | Subtracting patch means removes a constant offset; dividing by centred patch norms removes positive scale, when variance is nonzero. Normalization without mean subtraction does not remove offsets. | Szeliski §9.1; HC6b p.103
tracking | core | Why restrict a tracker's search around its previous position? | A motion prior reduces computation and ambiguous matches, but can miss a target that moves farther than the allowed search region. | HC3b pp.77,80,87
tracking | core | What is tracking by detection? | Detect or classify candidate regions in each frame and associate/update the target using an appearance model and motion constraints. | HC3b pp.89–106
tracking | core | What is tracking drift? | Progressive loss of the true target, often because incorrect self-labelled updates learn background or an occluder. | HC3b pp.89–106
tracking | core | What representation does mean-shift tracking often compare? | Target and candidate-region colour histograms, using a similarity measure; local updates seek a more similar candidate position. | HC3b p.122
tracking | breadth | What can make colour-histogram tracking robust and what can make it fail? | It tolerates some pixel rearrangement but loses spatial layout; similarly coloured backgrounds, large appearance change and occlusion can confuse it. | HC3b pp.78,122; Szeliski §9.4.4
tracking | breadth | How can descriptor-based tracking tolerate some occlusion better than one raw template? | Visible local descriptors may still match even when part of the target is hidden; geometric and object/background consistency are still needed. | HC3b pp.97–106

# Geometric transforms and stitching
geometry | core | What is a global image transform? | One parameterized coordinate mapping is applied across the image domain; it is not independently chosen for each pixel. | HC4a p.6
geometry | core | Give a homogeneous 2D translation matrix for column vectors. | T=[[1,0,tx],[0,1,ty],[0,0,1]]. It sends [x,y,1] to [x+tx,y+ty,1]. | HC4a pp.14–16
geometry | core | Give a homogeneous 2D scaling matrix. | S=diag(sx,sy,1). Uniform scaling has sx=sy; nonuniform scaling can change angles and shapes. | HC4a pp.8–10,16
geometry | core | What is a 2D affine transformation? | x'=A*x+t. Its homogeneous matrix has last row [0,0,1] after normalization and 6 free parameters in general. | HC4a pp.17–22
geometry | core | What is a 2D similarity transformation? | Rotation plus translation plus one uniform scale; ordinarily 4 degrees of freedom: angle, two translations and scale. | HC4a p.21
geometry | core | What is a 2D rigid/Euclidean transformation? | Rotation plus translation, with 3 degrees of freedom. It preserves Euclidean distances and angles. | HC4a p.21
geometry | core | Which properties does a nonsingular affine transform preserve? | Lines, parallelism and ratios of distances along a common line. It does not generally preserve angles, lengths or areas unchanged. | HC4a pp.17–18
geometry | core | What is a planar projective transformation or homography? | A nonsingular 3x3 homogeneous mapping between projective image-plane points, defined up to a nonzero scale. Dehomogenization makes its Cartesian mapping projective. | HC4a pp.20–22
geometry | core | Why does a homography have 8 degrees of freedom? | Its 9 matrix entries are defined only up to one common nonzero scale, leaving 8 independent parameters. | HC4a p.22
geometry | core | What do general nonsingular homographies preserve? | Incidence and collinearity: straight lines map to straight lines. They do not generally preserve Euclidean lengths, angles, parallelism or ordinary distance ratios. | HC1b p.28; HC4a pp.20–22
geometry | core | How do you compose two transforms H followed by T with column-vector coordinates? | Use T*H. The rightmost transform acts first; multiplication order generally matters. | HC4a p.19
geometry | core | What is forward image warping? | Iterate over source pixels, map their coordinates forward and place/splat their values in the destination. Discretization can leave holes or create collisions. | HC4a pp.24–27
geometry | core | What is backward/inverse image warping? | Iterate over destination pixels, inverse-map each location and sample/interpolate the source. Handle out-of-domain samples explicitly. | HC4a pp.28–31
geometry | core | Why is backward warping commonly used for raster images? | It determines a sample for each destination location within mapped source coverage, avoiding forward-mapping discretization holes. It does not create valid data outside coverage. | HC4a pp.28–31
geometry | core | What is nearest-neighbour interpolation? | Use the value of the nearest source grid location. It is simple but can look blocky and does not average neighbouring values. | HC4a p.29; Lab4
geometry | core | What is bilinear interpolation? | A weighted average of four surrounding source pixels, linear separately in x and y. Weights depend on fractional position and sum to one. | HC4a p.30; Lab4
geometry | core | Give bilinear interpolation weights for fractional offsets dx and dy. | Top-left:(1-dx)*(1-dy); top-right:dx*(1-dy); bottom-left:(1-dx)*dy; bottom-right:dx*dy, using I[y,x]. | HC4a p.30; Lab4
geometry | core | Under what two major conditions can two ideal views share one homography? | The viewed points lie on one scene plane, or the camera rotates around the same optical centre with no translation. | HC4a pp.34–35; S4
geometry | core | Why does translating a camera through a nonplanar scene cause stitching problems? | Image shifts depend on depth (parallax), so one homography generally cannot align all depths simultaneously. | HC4a pp.34–35; S4; Lab4
geometry | core | How many generic point pairs minimally determine a homography? | Four in general position, with no degeneracy such as three collinear points in a minimal configuration. Each pair supplies two scalar equations. | HC4a pp.46–55; S4
geometry | core | How many generic point pairs minimally determine a general affine transform? | Three pairs with noncollinear source points. The 6 parameters need independent coordinate constraints. | HC4a p.50; Lab4
geometry | core | Write the coordinate formula for homography H at finite source (x,y). | x'=(h11*x+h12*y+h13)/(h31*x+h32*y+h33); y'=(h21*x+h22*y+h23)/(h31*x+h32*y+h33), when the denominator is nonzero. | HC4a pp.47–52
geometry | core | What is DLT's homogeneous system for homography fitting? | Stack two linear constraints per correspondence to form A*h=0, where h contains the 9 entries of H. A has shape (2N,9). | HC4a pp.51–53
geometry | core | Why must homogeneous least squares constrain homography scale? | Otherwise h=0 trivially minimizes ||A*h||^2 but defines no mapping. A common constraint is ||h||_2=1. | HC4a p.54
geometry | core | Which eigenvector solves unit-norm homogeneous least squares for a homography? | The eigenvector of A.T*A with the smallest eigenvalue; equivalently the corresponding right singular vector of A. | HC4a p.55
geometry | core | What is reprojection error in image alignment? | The distance between an observed target point and the dehomogenized prediction from the fitted transform; specify one-way or symmetric error and units. | HC4a pp.46–55; Lab4
geometry | core | What are the main steps of a feature-based stitching pipeline? | Detect/describe keypoints; match descriptors; reject ambiguity; robustly estimate a transform; warp to a common canvas; blend overlaps. | HC4a pp.33–64; S4; Lab4
geometry | core | What is weighted image blending in an overlap? | Combine aligned intensities with normalized weights, such as I=w*I1+(1-w)*I2 with 0<=w<=1. A ramp can reduce visible seams. | HC4a pp.60–64; S4
geometry | core | Can blending repair arbitrary geometric misalignment? | No. It can soften seams but may create ghosting/double images if content is not aligned, especially with parallax or moving objects. | HC4a pp.60–64; Lab4
geometry | breadth | Why must panorama canvas offset be included in the warp matrix? | Negative/reference-image coordinates may need translation into valid canvas coordinates. With column vectors, source-to-canvas is T_canvas*H_source_to_reference. | Lab4; HC4a p.19

# Descriptors and matching
matching | core | What is the difference between keypoint detection and description? | Detection chooses repeatable locations and often scales/orientations; description encodes local appearance for comparisons. | HC4a pp.36–44; S4; Lab4
matching | core | What does SIFT stand for? | Scale-Invariant Feature Transform. Its usual pipeline includes scale-space detection, orientation assignment and a local gradient descriptor. | HC4a pp.41–44; S4
matching | breadth | Who is associated with SIFT and the commonly used ratio test? | David Lowe. | HC4a p.44; Szeliski §7.1.2
matching | core | What are the main SIFT construction stages? | Detect DoG scale-space extrema; refine/reject unstable points; assign dominant orientation; form normalized local gradient-orientation histograms. | HC4a pp.36–44; S4; Szeliski §7.1
matching | core | Why does SIFT use a scale-space detector? | To detect structures at corresponding characteristic scales after image/object rescaling, rather than using one fixed pixel-scale detector. | HC4a pp.36–41
matching | core | How does SIFT gain rotation robustness? | Assign a dominant local gradient orientation and express the descriptor's spatial/orientation bins relative to it. | HC4a p.42; S4
matching | core | How long is a standard SIFT descriptor and why? | 128 values: a 4x4 grid of subregions, each with an 8-bin gradient-orientation histogram. | S4; Szeliski §7.1.2 pp.434–436
matching | core | What weights contribute to the standard SIFT histogram entries? | Local gradient magnitudes with spatial Gaussian weighting, distributed into spatial/orientation bins rather than storing raw pixels. | HC4a p.42; S4; Szeliski §7.1.2
matching | core | How does normalization improve SIFT's illumination robustness? | Gradients remove a constant additive offset; vector normalization reduces a common positive gain. Clipping and renormalization further reduce dominant responses. | S4; Szeliski §7.1.2
matching | breadth | Is SIFT exactly invariant to every viewpoint, lighting or deformation change? | No. It is designed for scale/rotation robustness and some illumination/local deformation tolerance, not unlimited projective or nonuniform appearance invariance. | HC4a pp.36–44; Szeliski §7.1
matching | core | How does brute-force descriptor matching work? | Compute distances between a query descriptor and candidate descriptors, then select nearest candidates under the chosen metric. | Lab4; S4
matching | core | What is Lowe's nearest-neighbour ratio test? | Keep a proposed match when nearest distance d1 divided by second-nearest distance d2 is below a chosen threshold, with d2>0 and a declared strict/inclusive rule. | HC4a p.44; S4; Lab4
matching | core | Why can nearest absolute distance alone be misleading? | Repeated patterns can offer multiple nearly equally good alternatives; a small nearest distance does not prove a distinctive or correct match. | HC4a p.44
matching | core | What does a ratio d1/d2 close to one suggest? | Ambiguous descriptor matching: the best and next-best alternatives are similarly plausible. | HC4a p.44; Lab4
matching | core | Does passing a descriptor ratio test guarantee geometric correctness? | No. Use geometric constraints and robust estimation, such as a homography/affine RANSAC, to reject inconsistent matches. | HC4a pp.44–48; Lab4
matching | core | How must a ratio-test threshold change if distances are squared? | d1/d2<t is equivalent to D1/D2<t^2 for D=d^2. Comparing squared ratios to t itself changes the acceptance rule. | HC4a ratio test; prerequisite algebra
matching | breadth | What is geometric verification in image retrieval? | Check whether local feature matches agree with a plausible transform or other scene geometry, rather than ranking only by aggregate appearance similarity. | HC5a retrieval; HC4a; Szeliski §6.1
matching | breadth | Why are local descriptors useful under partial occlusion? | Some unoccluded regions can still provide matches even when the whole-image appearance changes. False matches and geometry still need checking. | HC3b pp.97–106; HC5a retrieval

# Clustering
clustering | core | What is k-means' usual objective? | Minimize the sum of squared Euclidean distances from data points to their assigned cluster centres for a chosen K. | HC5a pp.91–118
clustering | core | What are k-means' two alternating update steps? | Assign each point to its nearest centre, then replace each nonempty cluster's centre by the mean of its assigned points. Repeat until a stopping condition. | HC5a p.93
clustering | core | Why does k-means use the mean as each updated centre? | The arithmetic mean minimizes summed squared Euclidean distances within a fixed assigned cluster. | HC5a p.93; HC3a pp.83–85
clustering | core | Does k-means guarantee the global optimum? | No. It can converge to a local optimum and is sensitive to initialization; multiple starts can help. | HC5a pp.110–111
clustering | core | What choices strongly affect k-means results? | K, feature representation, feature scaling/metric, initialization and stopping/empty-cluster handling. | HC5a pp.91–118
clustering | core | What is the difference between k-means and k-nearest neighbours? | K-means is unsupervised clustering with K centres; k-NN is supervised prediction from the labels of nearby training examples. | HC5a clustering; HC4b pp.37–58
clustering | core | Why does adding pixel position to colour features change clustering? | It adds spatial distance to the metric, encouraging spatial compactness. Relative feature scales control its influence. | HC5a pp.91–93
clustering | core | Does colour-only k-means guarantee connected image regions? | No. Distant pixels with similar colour can get the same cluster label. Semantic regions and connected components are separate concepts. | HC5a pp.91–118
clustering | core | What is mean-shift clustering? | A mode-seeking density-based procedure that repeatedly moves a point/window to a local kernel-weighted mean until convergence. | HC3b pp.110–119
clustering | core | What is kernel bandwidth in mean shift? | The neighbourhood/scale over which density is estimated. It influences detail, attraction basins and the number of modes/clusters. | HC3b pp.110–119
clustering | core | Must mean shift specify a target number of clusters K? | Not in its basic form. Modes emerge from the data, kernel and bandwidth; a mode-merging rule can affect final grouping. | HC3b p.119
clustering | breadth | What is an attraction basin in mean shift? | The set of starting locations whose update trajectories converge to the same density mode. | HC3b p.119

# Classification
classification | core | What is Bag-of-Visual-Words (BoVW)? | Represent an image by counts or normalized counts of quantized local descriptors assigned to a learned visual vocabulary. | HC4b pp.23–28; S4
classification | core | How is a visual vocabulary/codebook usually built? | Extract training-image descriptors and cluster them, commonly using k-means; the cluster centres are visual words. | HC4b pp.25–28; S4
classification | core | What determines the dimension of a plain BoVW histogram? | The vocabulary size K, not the image size or number of detected descriptors. | HC4b pp.25–28; S4
classification | core | How is one descriptor assigned to a visual word in hard quantization? | Choose its nearest vocabulary centre under the specified descriptor metric. | HC4b pp.25–28; S4
classification | core | Why normalize visual-word counts? | Reduce dependence on the number of descriptors/image size, using a specified normalization such as L1 count proportions. | HC4b pp.25–28; S4
classification | core | What is a major limitation of plain BoVW? | It discards spatial layout and quantization loses descriptor detail; different arrangements can yield the same histogram. | HC4b pp.23–28; S4
classification | core | Should the BoVW vocabulary be fitted using held-out test images for ordinary evaluation? | No. Fit preprocessing on training data, select settings on validation, then evaluate the untouched test set to avoid leakage. | HC4b pp.55–58; Lab5
classification | core | What does a k-nearest-neighbour classifier do? | Find the k nearest labelled training examples and combine their labels, usually by majority vote with a declared tie/weight rule. | HC4b pp.37–53
classification | core | Give L1 and L2 feature distances. | L1=sum_j abs(xj-yj). L2=sqrt(sum_j (xj-yj)^2). Their rankings can differ in multiple dimensions. | HC4b pp.39,51–52
classification | core | What is the usual effect of increasing k in k-NN? | Smoother, less noisy decision boundaries, but potentially more bias/underfitting. Choose k using validation, not a universal rule. | HC4b pp.45–58
classification | core | Is k-NN training/prediction cost asymmetric? | It mainly stores training data, while naive prediction scans many examples. Per-query distance cost is roughly O(N*D) for N examples of dimension D. | HC4b p.40
classification | core | What is a hyperparameter? | A selected model/training setting such as k, vocabulary size, learning rate or architecture, rather than a parameter fitted by the training optimizer. | HC4b pp.55–58; Lab5
classification | core | What are the roles of train, validation and test splits? | Train fits parameters; validation selects hyperparameters/models; test estimates performance of the final selected pipeline on held-out examples. | HC4b pp.55–58; Lab5
classification | core | Why is tuning repeatedly on the test set invalid for a clean final estimate? | Model choices adapt to test outcomes, so the set is no longer an independent evaluation and its score can be optimistic. | HC4b p.56
classification | breadth | What is cross-validation? | Repeatedly train on some folds and validate on another, aggregating validation results to compare choices; keep a final test set separate. | HC4b pp.57–58
classification | core | Write a linear multiclass score function and its shapes. | s=W*x+b. For input dimension D and C classes: W is (C,D), x is (D,), b and s are (C,). | HC4b pp.62–65
classification | core | What does a linear classifier's decision boundary look like in feature space? | A hyperplane for a binary score threshold; pairwise equal class scores also give hyperplanes. It need not be linear in raw pixels if features are nonlinear. | HC4b pp.61–78
classification | core | What is the central SVM goal? | Find a separating decision boundary with a large margin, balancing margin/regularization against allowed violations in the soft-margin setting. | HC4b pp.69–78
classification | core | What are support vectors? | Training examples with nonzero dual weights in the SVM solution, typically on or inside the margin or misclassified in a soft-margin model. They determine its fitted boundary. | HC4b p.69; Szeliski §5.1.4
classification | core | Why use a soft-margin SVM? | To handle nonseparable/noisy data by penalizing margin violations instead of requiring a perfect hard-margin separation. | HC4b pp.69,74
classification | core | Write binary hinge loss for label y in {-1,+1} and score s. | max(0,1-y*s). A standard soft-margin SVM also includes weight-norm regularization. | HC4b p.74
classification | core | What does the kernel trick compute? | Inner products of implicit transformed features without explicitly constructing the full transformed vectors, allowing nonlinear boundaries in the original space. | HC4b pp.77–78

# Evaluation
metrics | core | Define TP, FP, FN and TN for a chosen positive class. | TP: actual positive predicted positive. FP: actual negative predicted positive. FN: actual positive predicted negative. TN: actual negative predicted negative. | HC4b pp.80–86; S4
metrics | core | What is accuracy? | Number correct divided by number evaluated; for binary predictions, (TP+TN)/(TP+FP+FN+TN). | HC4b pp.81–82
metrics | core | What is precision? | TP/(TP+FP): fraction of positive predictions that are correct. Handle a zero denominator using the stated evaluation rule. | HC4b p.83
metrics | core | What is recall? | TP/(TP+FN): fraction of actual positives detected. Also called sensitivity or true-positive rate. | HC4b p.84
metrics | core | What is F1? | Harmonic mean 2*P*R/(P+R), equivalently 2*TP/(2*TP+FP+FN) when defined. | HC4b p.80; S4
metrics | core | Why is F1 not the arithmetic mean of precision and recall? | Its harmonic mean penalizes imbalance more; one very small component makes F1 small even if the other is high. | HC4b p.80; S4
metrics | core | Why can accuracy be misleading for imbalanced classes? | Correct majority predictions can dominate the score while the minority class has very low or zero recall. | HC4b p.82
metrics | core | With confusion-matrix rows actual and columns predicted, where are FP and FN for class c? | TP is diagonal C[c,c]; FP is column-c sum minus TP; FN is row-c sum minus TP. | HC4b pp.80–86; S4
metrics | core | What is macro averaging across classes? | Compute each class's metric and average them equally, irrespective of class frequency. | HC4b evaluation; S4
metrics | core | What is micro averaging across classes? | Pool the relevant TP/FP/FN counts first, then compute the metric. For complete single-label multiclass evaluation, micro precision/recall/F1 equal accuracy. | HC4b evaluation; S4; prerequisite aggregation
metrics | core | What usually happens when a positive-score threshold is lowered? | More positive predictions: recall cannot decrease for a fixed ranking, while precision may change and need not be monotone. | HC4b pp.85–86
metrics | core | What is Precision@K in retrieval? | Number of relevant items among the first K results divided by K, assuming K results are available. It ignores all lower ranks. | HC5a p.19
metrics | core | What is Recall@K in retrieval? | Number of relevant items among the first K results divided by the total number of relevant database items. | HC5a pp.19–24; S4
metrics | core | What does a precision–recall curve vary? | The score/rank threshold, tracing the precision and recall obtained as more predictions or results are admitted. | HC4b p.86; HC5a evaluation
metrics | core | Give the raw uninterpolated ranking-AP formula. | AP=sum over relevant ranks k of Precision@k, divided by the total relevant/ground-truth count. Missing relevant items contribute zero. Use this only when that AP convention is specified. | HC5a pp.19–24,68–78; S4; SOURCE_NOTES
metrics | core | What is all-points interpolated AP? | Area under a precision envelope: at each recall use the maximum precision attainable at that recall or later, then sum recall increments times envelope precision. | HC5a pp.68–78; S4; SOURCE_NOTES
metrics | core | Why should an AP computation state the interpolation convention? | Raw ranking AP, all-points interpolation and 11-point/sampled-recall conventions can give different values on the same predictions. AP is not a uniquely specified arithmetic procedure without that choice. | HC5a evaluation; S4; SOURCE_NOTES
metrics | core | What is mean Average Precision (mAP) for detection classes at one IoU threshold? | The arithmetic mean of class AP values under the same declared matching and AP conventions. It is not precision averaged over raw boxes. | HC5a pp.68–78
metrics | core | How does mAP@0.5 differ from COCO-style averaged-IoU AP? | mAP@0.5 uses one matching threshold; COCO-style AP averages over thresholds 0.50,0.55,...,0.95 as well, using its defined sampled-recall evaluation. | HC5a p.78
metrics | core | How are detections matched to ground truth for AP? | Per class, process detections in descending score and match valid overlaps to unmatched ground-truth objects under the declared IoU rule. A ground-truth object can be claimed once. | HC5a pp.68–76
metrics | core | What happens to a duplicate high-IoU detection of an already matched object? | It is a false positive unless it can validly match another unmatched ground-truth object. A high overlap alone does not make every duplicate a TP. | HC5a pp.69–76
metrics | core | Must AP equal zero after one early false positive? | No. It lowers some precision values but later true positives can still contribute. AP summarizes the ranking and achieved recall under its convention. | HC5a pp.68–78
metrics | breadth | What is top-5 classification accuracy? | Fraction of examples whose true label appears among the model's five highest-scoring class labels. | HC4b p.34

# CNN mechanics
cnn | core | What are the key structural ideas in a CNN layer? | Local connectivity/receptive fields, tiling a filter across space and sharing its weights. Multiple filters produce multiple feature maps. | HC5b pp.22–43
cnn | core | What is a feature map? | The spatial array of responses for a learned filter/output channel; a layer's output stacks feature maps across channels. | HC5b pp.35–43,61–68
cnn | core | What does weight sharing mean in a convolution? | The same kernel parameters are used at different spatial positions, rather than learning independent weights at every location. | HC5b pp.22–24; S5
cnn | core | What is translation equivariance? | Shifting the input shifts the corresponding output representation under the specified operator. It is not an unchanged output. | HC5b pp.22–24; S5
cnn | core | What is translation invariance? | Output remains unchanged under translation. A raw convolution is generally equivariant, while aggregation/pooling can provide limited or additional invariance. | HC5b p.64; S5
cnn | core | Why do stride and boundaries limit exact convolutional equivariance? | Strides sample on a fixed grid, and padding/cropping can change boundary context. Only compatible translations/regions preserve the ideal relation. | HC5b pp.35–40; S5
cnn | core | How does a dense multi-channel 2D convolution make one output channel? | For each output location, sum spatial kernel products across all input channels and add that output channel's bias. | HC5b pp.66–68
cnn | core | What is the weight-tensor shape for a standard Cout-output, Cin-input 2D convolution in output-first notation? | (Cout,Cin,Kh,Kw). A library may store axes differently, but the parameter count is unchanged. | HC5b pp.66–68; S5
cnn | core | Give a standard dense 2D convolution's parameter count with bias. | Cout*(Kh*Kw*Cin+1). Stride, batch size and image spatial size do not change the count. | HC5b pp.66–73; S5
cnn | core | Give a fully connected layer's parameter count with bias. | Dout*(Din+1). Flattened input dimension includes every spatial position and channel fed to that layer. | S5 Exercise 1
cnn | core | What is the standard convolution/pooling output size per axis with no dilation? | floor((N+2*P-K)/S)+1, using symmetric padding P on each side, kernel K and stride S. | HC5b pp.25–40; S5
cnn | core | What padding preserves size for an odd K at stride 1? | P=(K-1)/2 on each side, for no dilation and standard symmetric padding. | HC5b pp.35–40; S5
cnn | core | What does a 1x1 convolution do? | Mixes input channels independently at each spatial location; it can change channel count without spatial mixing in that layer. | HC5b pp.70–73
cnn | core | What is the parameter count of a dense 1x1 convolution with bias? | Cout*(Cin+1). It is not parameter-free and is not necessarily an identity. | HC5b pp.70–73
cnn | core | Why use 1x1 layers in a bottleneck block? | Reduce channel count before an expensive spatial convolution, then expand again. This can reduce parameters and compute for suitable channel choices. | HC5b p.73; HC6a pp.107–111
cnn | core | Why add nonlinear activations between linear/affine layers? | A composition of affine layers without nonlinearities is still one affine mapping; nonlinearities allow more complex decision functions. | HC5b pp.44–59
cnn | core | What is ReLU? | ReLU(x)=max(0,x), applied elementwise. Its derivative is 1 for x>0 and 0 for x<0; define the zero-point convention when needed. | HC5b pp.44–59; S5
cnn | core | What is max pooling? | Replace each spatial window per channel by its maximum, often with stride to reduce resolution. It has no ordinary learned weights or biases. | HC5b pp.64,69,74–77
cnn | core | How does max-pooling backpropagation route gradients? | To the location(s) selected as maxima in the forward window, under the library's tie rule; other entries receive zero from that output. | HC5b p.69; S5
cnn | core | Is average pooling linear? Is max pooling linear? | Fixed average pooling is linear under a fixed boundary rule. Max pooling is generally nonlinear. Both can downsample. | HC5b pooling; prerequisite linearity
cnn | core | What is global average pooling? | Mean each feature channel across all spatial positions, producing one value per channel and no learned pooling parameters. | HC6a architecture diagrams; Lab5
cnn | core | What is a theoretical receptive field? | The input-region support that can influence a particular output unit through the network's connectivity. Distinguish it from observed/weighted effective influence. | HC5b pp.80–85; HC6a pp.6–9
cnn | core | What is the receptive-field side length of L stride-1 K-wide convolutions with no intervening strides? | 1+L*(K-1), ignoring boundaries. The simple formula changes when strides/pooling intervene. | HC5b p.82
cnn | core | Give the receptive-field recurrence that includes strides. | Start r=1,j=1. For kernel K and stride S: r_new=r+(K-1)*j; j_new=j*S. Apply in layer order, ignoring dilation unless specified. | HC5b pp.80–85; HC6a pp.6–9
cnn | core | How does a CNN typically change features across depth? | Early layers tend to represent simple local edges/textures; later layers combine them into more complex patterns, often with larger receptive fields. | HC5b pp.88–90
cnn | core | What is the multiply-accumulate count of a dense 2D convolution's forward kernel work? | Hout*Wout*Cout*Kh*Kw*Cin, excluding bias/nonlinearities. Counts of scalar FLOPs can be roughly twice this if multiply and add are counted separately. | HC5b p.68; S5
cnn | core | What is a standard dense 3D convolution's parameter count with bias? | Cout*(Kt*Kh*Kw*Cin+1), where Kt is temporal/depth support. Frame count is not Cin unless frames were explicitly packed as channels. | S5 Exercise 5
cnn | core | How is true video 3D convolution different from independent framewise 2D convolution? | A 3D kernel mixes across temporal/depth neighbours as well as spatial positions. Independent 2D processing does not itself combine frames. | S5 Exercise 5
cnn | breadth | Does factorizing a 3D convolution into spatial 2D plus temporal 1D always reduce parameters? | No. The count depends on intermediate channels: Kh*Kw*Cin*Cmid+Kt*Cmid*Cout, plus chosen biases. It can reduce parameters for suitable dimensions. | S5 Exercise 5; SOURCE_NOTES
cnn | core | What is the gradient of a shared kernel weight in a CNN? | Sum upstream-gradient times the corresponding input sample over all output positions (and batch examples) using that weight. | S5 Exercise 3
cnn | core | Why can input-gradient contributions overlap during convolution backpropagation? | One input pixel can affect several output windows. Its gradient is the sum of all such contributions, not just one patch. | S5 Exercise 3

optimization | core | What is gradient descent's basic parameter update? | theta_new=theta_old-learning_rate*gradient(loss,theta). A positive learning rate moves against the local gradient. | S5; Lab5
optimization | core | What is stochastic/minibatch gradient descent? | Estimate the loss gradient from an example or minibatch and update parameters, rather than using the whole dataset for every step. | Lab5 tutorial and assignment

optimization | core | Why initialize interchangeable hidden-unit weights randomly? | Break symmetry so units can learn different features, rather than producing identical activations and gradients. | Lab5 assignment Tasks 5,8

optimization | core | What are vanishing gradients? | Backpropagated derivatives become very small through many transformations, so early parameters receive little useful update signal. | HC6a pp.100–103; S5; Lab5
optimization | core | What are exploding gradients? | Backpropagated derivatives become very large, causing unstable updates or numerical overflow. | S5; Lab5
optimization | core | What methods can help vanishing/exploding-gradient problems? | Suitable initialization, normalization, non-saturating activations and residual paths; gradient clipping specifically limits large gradients. These are mitigations, not universal guarantees. | S5 Exercise 3; Lab5
optimization | core | What does gradient clipping mainly address? | Excessive gradient magnitude, helping control exploding updates. It does not restore information from a zero/vanished gradient. | S5 Exercise 3
optimization | core | What is the dying-ReLU problem? | A unit stays at nonpositive preactivation for all relevant inputs, giving zero output and no gradient through its ReLU branch under the usual convention. | S5 Exercise 3

optimization | core | What is underfitting? | A model or training process fails to capture useful patterns even in training data, due to limited capacity, strong constraints or unsuccessful optimization. | Lab5 tutorial; HC6a pp.100–101

optimization | core | What can reduce overfitting? | Suitable data augmentation, regularization, early stopping using validation, more representative data or reduced model complexity. Evaluate the effects, not a guaranteed ranking. | Lab5 tutorial and assignment

optimization | core | What is dropout at a high level? | Randomly omit activations during training under a chosen convention to reduce co-adaptation; normal evaluation uses its defined deterministic/scaled behaviour. | Lab5 network code; YOLOv4 §2.2

detection | core | What are IoU's ordinary value range and endpoints? | 0 to 1: zero for disjoint regions; one for identical regions of positive area. | HC5a p.62
detection | core | What is greedy hard non-maximum suppression? | Keep the highest-scoring remaining box, suppress lower-scoring boxes with excessive IoU against it, then repeat. Use a declared threshold and class policy. | HC5a pp.63–67
detection | core | Is confidence thresholding the same as NMS? | No. Thresholding removes low-scoring candidates; NMS removes redundant overlapping candidates using scores and overlap. Pipelines often use both. | HC5a pp.63–67; HC6a p.62
detection | core | Does a box suppressed by greedy NMS suppress other boxes later? | No. Suppression is based on boxes actually kept in the greedy procedure; overlap is not transitively propagated through a discarded box. | HC5a pp.63–67
detection | core | Why can hard NMS fail in crowded scenes? | Different genuine objects can have high box overlap, so one object's high-scoring box can suppress another object's valid detection. | HC5a p.67

detection | core | How does original R-CNN compute region features? | Generate external region proposals, warp/crop each region and run the CNN separately for each, then classify and refine boxes. | HC5b pp.100–124
detection | core | What are major original R-CNN inefficiencies? | Repeated CNN work for overlapping crops and a multistage training/caching pipeline; the shared-image computation of later variants improves this. | HC5b pp.118–124
detection | core | What changes in Fast R-CNN? | Compute one full-image convolutional feature map, extract fixed-size region features with RoI pooling, and jointly predict classes and box refinements. External proposals remain. | HC5b pp.125–130

detection | core | What does an RPN predict? | Objectness and box-regression offsets for candidate anchors at feature-map locations; its proposals feed the detector's second stage. | HC6a pp.19–38
detection | core | What is an anchor box? | A predefined reference box shape/scale at a location, refined by predicted offsets and assigned objectness/class information. Its definition depends on the detector version. | HC6a pp.56–60
detection | core | What is the difference between one-stage and two-stage detection? | One-stage predicts dense boxes/classes directly from image features; two-stage first selects proposals then processes proposal features for final predictions. Both can share a backbone. | HC6a pp.54–72; S5

detection | core | What is original YOLO v1's box-confidence convention? | Pr(object)*IoU(predicted box,ground truth). At training, no-object targets are zero; an object box's confidence relates to its IoU. This is version-specific. | HC6a pp.69–72; SOURCE_NOTES
detection | core | What is the original YOLO v1 class-specific detection score? | Pr(class|object)*Pr(object)*IoU, using its specified box-confidence convention. Do not assume every later detector has the same IoU factor. | HC6a pp.69–72
detection | core | How many candidate boxes does an SxS grid with B boxes per cell produce? | S*S*B before filtering. Output scalar count is a different question and depends on the detector's channel convention. | HC6a pp.56–62
detection | core | How does an SSD-type detector handle different object scales? | Predict from multiple feature-map resolutions with reference/default boxes of varying sizes and aspect ratios. | HC6a pp.40–63; Szeliski §6.3
detection | core | What is a detector backbone, neck and head? | Backbone extracts features; neck combines/transforms features, often across scales; head predicts object classes, boxes and related outputs. | S5 Exercise 4; YOLOv4 §2.1
detection | core | What is the role of a Feature Pyramid Network (FPN)? | Combine coarse high-level semantic features with finer spatial feature maps, using top-down and lateral paths, for multiscale detection. | HC6a pp.40–51; S5
detection | breadth | What does the YOLOv4 paper call a bag of freebies? | Training methods that improve accuracy without increasing inference cost, such as suitable augmentation or training-loss changes. | YOLOv4 §2.2; S5

# 3D vision
stereo | core | What is epipolar geometry? | The geometry relating corresponding points in two views, defined by the two camera centres and a scene point. It constrains a match to an epipolar line. | HC6b pp.50–65
stereo | core | What is an epipolar plane? | A plane containing both camera centres and a 3D point. Its intersection with each image plane gives that point's epipolar line. | HC6b pp.50–53
stereo | core | What is an epipole? | The projection of one camera centre into the other camera's image. It may lie inside, outside or at infinity in the image plane. | HC6b pp.50–63
stereo | core | Why does epipolar geometry reduce a 2D correspondence search to 1D? | A point in one image restricts the matching point in the other to a line, rather than any image location. Appearance still selects among points along that line. | HC6b pp.50–65
stereo | core | Write the pixel-coordinate epipolar constraint. | x2.T*F*x1=0 for homogeneous image points. F maps x1 to line l2=F*x1 in image 2. | HC6b pp.53–55
stereo | core | What are the dimensions, rank and degrees of freedom of a generic fundamental matrix? | 3x3, rank 2, 7 degrees of freedom after scale and rank constraints, for nondegenerate two-view geometry. | HC6b pp.54–55
stereo | core | Does F map a point to its unique matching point? | No. It maps a point to an epipolar line; appearance and other constraints are needed to find the match on that line. | HC6b pp.54–65
stereo | breadth | What is the essential matrix? | The calibrated two-view epipolar matrix for normalized camera coordinates. For relative pose X2=R*X1+t and constraint y2.T*E*y1=0, E=[t]_x*R, where [t]_x*v=t cross v. | HC6b pp.53–55
stereo | breadth | How are essential and fundamental matrices related? | F=K2^(-T)*E*K1^(-1), with E=K2.T*F*K1, under x2.T*F*x1=0 and corresponding calibrated coordinates. | HC6b pp.53–55
stereo | breadth | How many degrees of freedom does a generic essential matrix have? | Five: three relative-rotation parameters and two translation-direction parameters; translation magnitude is not observable from the epipolar relation. | HC6b p.55
stereo | core | Why is pure camera rotation degenerate for ordinary stereo depth triangulation? | It supplies no translation baseline or depth-dependent parallax; correspondences determine ray rotations but not distance along each ray. | HC4a p.34; HC6b pp.114–124
stereo | core | What is stereo rectification? | Warp both images so corresponding epipolar lines align, usually as horizontal scanlines; this simplifies matching to row-wise search. | HC6b pp.79–90
stereo | core | Does stereo rectification guarantee identical images or zero disparity? | No. It aligns epipolar lines; horizontal disparity still carries depth information, and occlusion/appearance differences remain. | HC6b pp.79–90
stereo | core | What is disparity in the convention used by the numerical practice questions? | d=uL-uR for an ideal rectified pair with equal principal-point coordinates. Other slide/camera conventions can reverse the sign; always check. | HC6b pp.85–90; SOURCE_NOTES
stereo | core | Write ideal rectified-stereo depth from disparity. | Z=f*B/d, with f in pixels, d in pixels, B in physical length units and a compatible sign/principal-point convention. | HC6b pp.84–90; Szeliski §12.1
stereo | core | How do depth and disparity relate? | Disparity is inversely proportional to depth for fixed focal length/baseline. Nearer points have larger disparity magnitude. | HC6b pp.85–90
stereo | core | Why are distant stereo depths sensitive to small disparity errors? | Z=fB/d gives sensitivity magnitude fB/d^2; small d leads to a large depth change per pixel of disparity error. | HC6b disparity; prerequisite calculus
stereo | core | What are common stereo matching difficulties? | Textureless/repeated patterns, occlusion, specular or lighting differences, depth boundaries and ambiguous correspondences. | HC6b pp.87–103
stereo | core | What is the stereo matching-window-size trade-off? | Small windows preserve detail but can be ambiguous/noisy; large windows stabilize matching but mix depths and lose boundary detail. | HC6b p.102
stereo | core | What are data and smoothness terms in stereo energy minimization? | Data term rewards good appearance matches for selected disparities; smoothness term encourages neighbouring disparities to be compatible, with care at real discontinuities. | HC6b pp.91–99
stereo | core | What can an excessively strong stereo smoothness penalty do? | Force nearly constant disparity and oversmooth real depth boundaries, sacrificing valid image-match evidence. | HC6b pp.95–96
stereo | breadth | What is triangulation? | Estimate a 3D point from corresponding image observations and camera geometry, ideally intersecting viewing rays; noisy rays require an approximate fit. | HC6b pp.114–128
stereo | core | What is Structure from Motion (SfM)? | Jointly estimate camera poses/motion and scene structure from overlapping images of a largely static scene. | HC6b pp.113–118
stereo | core | What is bundle adjustment? | Joint nonlinear refinement of camera parameters and 3D points to minimize reprojection error over observations, with appropriate gauge/reference constraints. | HC6b pp.108–110,123–129
stereo | core | What is reprojection error in bundle adjustment? | Difference between an observed image feature and the projected estimate of its 3D point using that view's camera parameters. | HC6b pp.109–110
stereo | core | What global ambiguity remains in ordinary calibrated monocular SfM without a metric reference? | Global scale, along with an arbitrary coordinate frame. Images alone do not make the reconstruction's units metres; unrestricted uncalibrated reconstruction can have a larger projective ambiguity. | HC6b pp.117–118
stereo | core | What is multi-view stereo (MVS) relative to SfM? | Densify scene geometry using multiple views and estimated/calibrated cameras; SfM usually supplies poses and sparse structure. | HC6b pp.104,112–118
stereo | breadth | What are main incremental SfM steps? | Initialize a suitable two-view reconstruction; triangulate; refine; register new views from 2D–3D matches; triangulate new points; repeat adjustment and outlier filtering. | HC6b pp.120–133
stereo | breadth | What is Perspective-n-Point (PnP) used for in incremental SfM? | Estimate a camera's pose from correspondences between known 3D points and their 2D image observations, under the specified camera calibration. | HC6b pp.125–128
stereo | breadth | How do global and hierarchical SfM differ at a high level? | Global methods estimate multi-camera relations jointly; hierarchical methods reconstruct subsets and merge them, often with similarity transforms. | HC6b pp.141–149
stereo | breadth | What is shape from silhouette and the visual hull? | Intersect silhouette viewing cones from known cameras to obtain a visual hull. Concavities invisible in all silhouettes cannot generally be recovered. | HC6b pp.22–24
stereo | breadth | What cue is used in shape from texture? | Changes in the appearance/density/foreshortening of an assumed repetitive texture can indicate surface orientation and depth variation. | HC6b pp.18–19
stereo | breadth | What cue is used in shape from focus? | The focus setting with highest local sharpness/frequency response relates to depth, under a calibrated camera/focus model. | HC6b p.20
stereo | breadth | What cue is used in shape from shadows? | Shadow geometry provides shape/height constraints when light and viewing geometry are sufficiently known or constrained. | HC6b p.21
